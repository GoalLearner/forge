You watch the ceiling fan trace its slow, useless circles. Three blades. Three days since the funeral. The fan has wobbled for years — Marcus always said he'd fix it. Now the wobble is permanent, a monument to every unkept promise, and you can't stop staring at it.

The bedroom door is open.

You didn't open it.

The hallway beyond breathes — a long, damp exhale that stirs the dust along the baseboards. It carries a smell you know: his shampoo, his sweat, the faint copper undertow of the hospital sheets they wrapped him in at the end.

"Close the door," you say. Your voice is rust.

The door doesn't close.

You understand now that grief is not a feeling. It is a *presence*. It moved into your home the moment the monitor flatlined, and it has been rearranging the furniture of your mind ever since. It wears his clothes. It sleeps on his side of the bed — you know because you woke at 3:17 a.m. to the mattress sagging beside you, to the warmth of something that wasn't there.

You pulled back the sheets. The impression of a body remained, indented in memory foam that should have rebounded. It hasn't. It's getting *deeper*.

Tonight you follow the breathing into the hallway.

The bathroom light is on. You find yourself standing in the doorway, and there is a figure at the sink — back turned, shoulders familiar in that threadbare gray t-shirt. It is brushing its teeth with his toothbrush, a rhythmic wet scrubbing that goes on too long, much too long, until you realize the sound has changed from bristles-on-enamel to something softer, wetter.

The figure turns.

It has your face. But worn wrong — your features stretched over something larger beneath, something that studied you in your sleep and practiced in mirrors while you wept. Its mouth is full of teeth that aren't yours, and it is grinning around the toothbrush that is no longer a toothbrush but a fingerbone, bleach-white and worn at the knuckle.

"Marcus is still here," it says, and the voice comes from your throat but not your lungs, the words pushed up from somewhere deep in the plumbing of the house.

The worst part — the part you will never, ever tell anyone — is that you don't scream. You don't run. You just stand there, slack and grateful, because for the first time in three days the apartment doesn't feel empty.

It hands you a cup of water the color of amniotic fluid. "Drink."

You drink.

The thing wearing your face smiles wider, skin splitting at the corners, and you understand that grief doesn't heal. Grief *ferments*. It cultures something in the dark of you, something patient and cellular. Given enough time, enough sleepless nights and untouched meals, it will grow a new version of you — one that doesn't miss anyone, because it was never capable of love to begin with.

You wake in bed. Sunlight. The ceiling fan wobbles. You don't remember walking back. Your hands smell of dirt and there is soil caked under your nails, dark and cold. The bedroom door is closed.

From the other side, Marcus knocks.

*Three times.*

You smile, because you know: if you let it in, you won't have to grieve anymore. That's the bargain. That's always been the bargain.
