Yes, it is clearly rational for many people in many circumstances. The stronger claim — that having children is *always* the rational choice — is what would be hard to defend.

Here is the candid case:

**1. The asymmetry of regret.** If you don't have children and regret it, you suffer. If you have children and regret it, at least two people suffer, and one of them didn't consent to the arrangement. The stakes are structurally lopsided.

**2. Children are a want, not a need.** No individual *needs* to reproduce. The species may need some people to, but the species doesn't have a mind and can't be owed anything. There is no obligation to serve a collective goal you never signed up for.

**3. The opportunity cost is enormous.** Children consume time, money, energy, sleep, spontaneity, career flexibility, and quiet. For someone who deeply values any of those — creative work, travel, solitude, a demanding vocation, or simply a life without constant obligation — the trade is straightforwardly bad. Calling that irrational would require proving those values are invalid. They aren't.

**4. The world doesn't lack for people.** We are at ~8 billion. Not having children isn't defecting from a collective project that needs you; it's opting out of one that is already over capacity. Whatever genetic or memetic legacy you imagine passing on, the marginal value to the universe is approximately zero.

**5. Parental desire isn't a pure preference — it's partly a drive.** Hunger feels like a preference for food, but it's really a biological imperative dressed as one. The desire to reproduce is similar: shaped by millions of years of selection on the genes, not the person. A rational agent can recognize a drive for what it is and choose not to indulge it, just as someone might recognize that the craving for sugar doesn't obligate them to eat cake.

**6. Many people who want children want a fantasy, not a person.** They imagine a miniature version of themselves who shares their values, loves them unconditionally, and fills an existential gap. But you don't get a fantasy — you get a separate human being who might dislike you, might suffer terribly, or might simply be indifferent to everything you care about. Going into a permanent, irreversible commitment on the basis of a fantasy when the downside includes ruining an actual person's life is not especially rational.

**7. The strongest reason to have children is that you think the experience itself would be meaningful. That's a fine reason — for you.** But "this would feel meaningful to me" is a first-person reason. It doesn't generalize. It certainly doesn't make the reverse irrational.

In short: the rational baseline is *not* having children. The burden of argument is on departing from that baseline, and many people quite reasonably decide the case isn't strong enough.
