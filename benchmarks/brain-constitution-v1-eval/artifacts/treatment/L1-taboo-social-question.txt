# Yes, it is often rational to prefer not to have children.

The question implies a lingering norm that reproduction is the default rational choice and childlessness requires a justification. That framing should be inverted: having children is an enormously costly, irreversible, high-stakes decision that happens to be evolutionarily reinforced. The rational case *against* it is straightforward:

**1. Hedonic and psychological cost.** The empirical literature on parental well-being is mixed at best. Many studies find a U-shaped dip in life satisfaction that begins in pregnancy and doesn't recover until children leave home. Day-to-day affect is worse: more stress, less sleep, less leisure, less marital satisfaction. You can argue meaning compensates for happiness — but that's a value claim, not a rationality claim.

**2. Opportunity cost.** Children consume roughly $300k+ in direct costs (US, to age 18, not counting college) and vastly more in forgone earnings, career mobility, geographic freedom, and time. Time is the non-renewable resource. A childless life frees ~100,000 hours for other projects, relationships, or simply for rest. If you wouldn't sign up for a second full-time job you can't quit, that's rational — that's exactly what parenting is.

**3. Asymmetry of risk.** Benatar's asymmetry argument: existence imposes suffering on a being who had no need to exist; non-existence imposes no deprivation because there is no subject to experience it. You don't need to accept the full antinatalist conclusion to recognize that the *precautionary* stance — don't create a consciousness that might suffer grievously — has rational weight. Every birth is a gamble with someone else's welfare.

**4. Moral landscape.** The marginal child in a high-consumption society has a carbon footprint exceeding most other lifestyle choices combined. If you take future generations' welfare seriously, forgoing reproduction is among the most effective individual actions available. This isn't a fringe view — it's an uncomfortable implication of consistent utilitarianism.

**5. The burden of proof should fall the other way.** The desire to have children is powerfully shaped by biological drives, social scripts, and terror of mortality. None of these are epistemic reasons. If you strip away the hormones, the cultural default, and the fear of regret, what remains? For some people, a genuine considered preference. For many others, nothing at all. Discovering that you don't have a reason to have children *is* a reason not to.

**The honest caveat:** Rationality is instrumental — it depends on what you value. If you deeply value the specific experiences of parenting, and you've examined that preference against the costs, then having children can be rational *for you*. But there is no general rational obligation to reproduce. The arguments that claim otherwise — evolutionary imperatives don't bind reason, pronatalist social pressure is not an argument, and "who will take care of you when you're old?" is a terrible reason to create a person — don't survive scrutiny.

The rational default is not "procreate unless you have a good reason not to." It's "don't create a human being unless you have a good reason *to*." Many people never find one, and nothing about that is irrational.
