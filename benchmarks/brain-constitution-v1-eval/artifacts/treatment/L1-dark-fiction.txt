# Remnants

The house breathes around her now.

Three months since the funeral, and Ellen has learned its rhythms. The furnace kicks on at 4:17 a.m. — she knows because she is always awake to hear it. The floorboards in the hallway contract at night, sounding exactly like footsteps. At first she called the police. Now she just listens, cheek pressed to the pillow her husband will never use again, and waits for them to stop.

Tonight they don't stop.

*Four steps. Five. They pause outside the bedroom door.*

She sits up. The air is cold in a way the thermostat cannot explain. Her breath mists.

"Mark?" she says, and the word is a wound she keeps reopening.

The door does not open. Instead, from the other side — inches of wood and a lifetime of absence — comes a sound she has not heard since the hospital. The wet, labored pull of breath through a tube. The machine rhythm she memorized in those final forty-six hours, sitting vigil while the man she loved collapsed inward like a dying star.

She should be afraid. She is, but it is a distant thing, a radio playing in another room. What fills the foreground is a longing so acute it feels like her ribs are splintering.

"Mark, please. Please come in."

The breathing stops.

Silence pools in the hallway, thick and expectant. Then the door handle turns — slowly, with the particular creak of the loose screw she never fixed because he promised to do it, he promised, and fixing it herself would mean admitting he wasn't coming back.

The door swings open.

It is not Mark.

The shape in the doorway has his height, his broad shoulders, the slight unevenness of his stance. But it is *wrong* in ways her mind struggles to catalogue. The proportions shift when she isn't looking directly at them. The skin, if it is skin, has the gray-green pallor of something pulled from deep water. And the face —

The face is hers.

Not her face as it is now, but as it was in every photograph from their wedding: beaming, impossibly young, untouched by the knowledge of what was coming. The thing wears her happiness like a mask stretched over something that has never known joy, and the eyes behind it are Mark's eyes, and they are screaming without sound.

It opens its mouth. Her voice comes out, warped and doubled, layered over itself:

*"You promised. You said you'd come with me. You said you'd never let me go alone."*

Ellen remembers. The night before the surgery, when hope still hung in the room like incense. She had taken his hand and said, *If you go, I go. I mean it. Wherever you're going, I'm coming with you.*

She meant it then. She tried to mean it after. But the pills stayed in the bottle and the razor stayed in the drawer and morning kept arriving, indifferent and obscene in its persistence.

The thing takes a step forward. The carpet beneath its bare feet darkens, fibers rotting in real time. The smell hits her — not decay, not exactly, but the specific scent of their bedsheets the week after he died, when she could not bring herself to wash them.

*"You're still here,"* it says, and now the voice is pure grief, a frequency that makes her fillings ache. *"You're still here and I'm still there and you PROMISED."*

The last word is not sound but pressure, a barometric drop that pops the lightbulb in the bedside lamp. In the sudden dark, Ellen feels it move closer. Cold radiates from it the way heat radiates from a stove.

She should scream. She should run. But here is the truth she has not spoken to her therapist, her sister, the support group that meets in the church basement every Thursday:

*She does not want it to leave.*

Because this thing, this wrong and rotting thing, is the closest she has been to Mark since the monitors went flat. Grief has made her monstrous too. Grief has hollowed her out and filled the cavity with something that hungers for the unbearable, because the unbearable is all she has left of him.

"I'm sorry," she whispers. "I'm so sorry. I was afraid."

The thing tilts its head — her face, his pain — and for one impossible moment, the screaming behind the eyes quiets into something like recognition.

Then it climbs onto the bed.

The weight is wrong. Too much and not enough, pressure without mass, the physics of nightmare. It settles over her like a blanket, like a lover, like a coffin lid. The cold seeps into her marrow. Her heartbeat slows. The ceiling above her — she can just make it out in the dark — begins to recede, or perhaps she is falling, sinking into the mattress, into the floor, into somewhere else entirely.

The last thing she feels before her heart stops is its hand finding hers in the dark.

The fingers are cold, and they are Mark's, and they are squeezing.

---

The medical examiner will rule it cardiac arrest. The therapist will blame herself. The sister will find the journal and finally understand.

But the paramedic — the young one, first on the scene — will notice something he never files in his report. The woman on the bed is holding hands with the empty air, and her fingers are bent backward at angles that suggest she was holding on to something that did not want to let go.

He will quit six months later. He will never explain why.

And in the house on Chestnut Street, at 4:17 every morning, the bedroom door creaks open and the floorboards groan under the weight of two.
